River Raid

Designed and programmed by Carol Shaw. A tribute you can play, and the story of rebuilding it instruction by instruction.

A river from sixteen bits

The river in River Raid is never stored. There isn't room: the whole game, code and graphics, fits in a 4,096-byte cartridge. Instead, every stretch of river is built just before it scrolls onto the screen, from a 16-bit random number generator that starts from the same seed, $A814, every game. That is why the river is endless and why it is the same river every time you play.

Each piece of river is a block 32 scanlines tall. The generator picks the next bank shape from fifteen patterns, decides whether an island begins or ends, and drops one object into it: a ship, a helicopter, later a jet, a fuel depot, or a house on the bank. Thirty-two blocks make a section, and every section ends with a road and a bridge. Odd-numbered sections run straight; even ones wind.

Everything on the river

Every object is a single 8-pixel-wide player sprite, drawn on alternating scanlines from two tables so it gets full vertical detail. Point values come straight from the cartridge's score table.

Tanker 30

Starts moving at random and patrols, turning back when it touches a bank. Where the river is too narrow for a ship, the cartridge swaps it for a helicopter.

Helicopter 60

The rotor is two alternating shapes, swapped every other frame. Patrols like the ship.

Jet 100

Appears from level three on, crosses the whole screen without stopping and wraps around at the edge.

Fuel 80

Fly over it to refuel. Shoot it and you lose the fuel but gain the points. Higher levels place fewer depots.

House 0

Scenery on the banks and islands. The only object on the river that scores nothing when you shoot it.

You +1 life / 10,000

Push up to speed up, pull back to slow down. The fuel gauge drains the whole time.

Carol Shaw

Carol Shaw was one of the first women to design and program video games professionally. At Atari she wrote 3-D Tic-Tac-Toe and Video Checkers for the 2600. At Activision she made River Raid, released in 1982 and one of the best-loved games on the system.

Reading the code, the craft shows in the details: the river's banks and the objects share a single random stream, so the same seed rebuilds the same world. The score lives directly in the pointers to the digit graphics. And the kernel checks collisions with the TIA's hardware latches once per block, so there is no collision arithmetic at all.

Rebuilding it $F000–$FFFF

This browser port does not emulate the Atari. It is a translation: each 6502 routine of the cartridge rewritten in JavaScript, one instruction per line, working on the same 128 bytes of RAM at the same addresses. To prove each translation correct, a small 6502 interpreter runs the real cartridge next to it, and every frame is compared byte for byte.
